Chapter 22

Service Monitoring

Key axiom: "If you aren't monitoring it, you aren't managing it." (adapted from "If you can't measure it, you can't manage it"). Monitoring is used to detect and fix problems, find their source, predict and avoid future problems, and show SA achievements.

The two primary types

Historical monitoring Real-time monitoring
Purpose Long-term uptime, usage, performance statistics Alerts SAs to a failure as soon as it happens
Components Collecting the data + viewing the data Monitoring component (notices failure) + alerting component (tells someone)
Output Conclusions and graphs (e.g., "99.99% uptime last year") Alerts / pages / tickets
Style of SA work Proactive Improves reactive SA work
Storage needs Large (lots of disk, databases, processing) Minimal (last result, time since last status change)
Example tools Cricket, Orca (also MRTG) Nagios, Big Brother

Goal of real-time monitoring: SAs notice outages before customers do. The two types are usually done by different systems.

Warnings about monitoring itself

  • Bandwidth: rule of thumb — monitoring should use no more than 1% of available bandwidth. On slow links, use remote monitoring stations or a trap-based system (device notifies on failure) instead of polling-based (monitor checks periodically).
  • CPU/memory: test failure modes (monitoring server down, network outage). When switching systems, turn off the old one.
  • Security: monitoring systems can be abused or spoofed; use strong authentication. SNMPv1 has weak authentication.

22.1.1 Historical Monitoring

  • Collects data by polling at intervals; used for graphs, finding past minor problems, and monitoring SLA conformance.
  • Uses: capacity planning (when to upgrade network, memory, CPU, disks), usage-based billing, anomaly detection, reports to management, budget justification.
  • Disk use is reduced by:
    • Condensing = replacing detailed data with averages (5-minute data → hourly averages saves ~90% storage).
    • Expiring = deleting old data (or archiving to DVD/tape).
  • Trade-off: condensing/expiring reduces detail and historical perspective. How you use the data decides how much to keep (e.g., monthly billing → keep full detail for a few years).
  • Polling should be configurable and use standard mechanisms: SNMPv2, ICMP echo (ping), TCP connections with pattern matching.
  • Record latency — a very slow service is practically the same as one that doesn't respond.
  • Need both custom tests and many predefined tests.
  • Output = graphs with clear units on each axis; graphs are good documentation for management.

22.1.2 Real-Time Monitoring

  • Must both poll and receive alerts (traps) from systems.
  • Send alerts to multiple recipients via several mechanisms (email, paging, phone, trouble tickets) — one person's pager may be dead.
  • Monitors two things:
    • Availability monitoring = detecting failures of hosts, apps, devices, interfaces, connections.
    • Capacity monitoring = detecting when a component is or is about to be overloaded (CPU, memory, disk, swap, ports, users...).
  • Most important components: the notification mechanism and the site's processes for handling alerts.

SNMP (Simple Network Monitoring Protocol)

  • GET = ask a device for a variable's value; PUT = change a variable; trap = "reply when a variable crosses a value."
  • MIB = a group of related variables (standard MIBs exist for Ethernet, DSL, disks, printers, CPUs...).
  • Weakness: one variable per packet → many packets; data encoded in complex ASN.1 (2002 router flaw).
  • Password = community string; defaults public (GET) and private (PUT). SNMPv2/v3 encrypt it.
  • Five recommendations: (1) accept SNMP only from specific IP ranges (one per redundant monitoring center); (2) use SNMPv3, encrypt community strings; (3) change community strings once a year with a transition period; (4) automate a weekly audit trying old/default passwords from inside and outside the ranges; (5) track vendor security bulletins.

Alerting mechanism

  • Must not depend on any component of the system being monitored.
  • Email is popular but should not be the only mechanism (can fail, can be delayed). Paging can be spied on — never send passwords.
  • Need a written alert-handling policy: who gets alerts, coordination, escalation path, frequency, severity.
  • Capacity warnings → open a ticket only when the state changes from acceptable to unacceptable. Critical outages → page the on-call person repeatedly.
  • Messages must be clear ("web server on www-20 is not responding", not raw IPs/OIDs) but must not make wrong assumptions ("not responding" vs "not running").
  • Case "I'm Hot! I'm Wet!" — HVAC alarm phoned the SA's home; wife thought it was a prank. Lesson: test your alerting system and inform those who might receive it.

Escalation — policy on how long a problem may persist before passing to another person (usually a manager); covers vacations or non-response. Acknowledging an alarm = "snooze button"; without it SAs turn alarms off and forget to reset them.

Active monitoring systems — detect and automatically fix known problems (e.g., reset a modem port).

  • Can only give a temporary fix, not fix the root cause; must report what it did and open a ticket.
  • Can make diagnosis harder; SAs may get lazy → entropy sets in.
  • Case log rotation "fix" — debug cron job filled /var/mail; auto-rotation wiped all logs to zero length.
  • Can't fix physical failures (no paper/ink) or multi-host problems.
  • Security risk: privileged access to many machines makes it a target; limit what it can do.

22.2 The Icing

  • Accessibility: any SA should be able to add items to monitor, not just the 1–2 who set it up. Needs good documentation, especially preferred defaults.
  • Pervasive monitoring: monitor everything (beyond desktops) by building it into the installation process; if not automatable, auto-generate a help ticket.
  • Device discovery: detects new devices added to the network — catches ones that fell through the cracks; saves debugging time.
  • End-to-end tests: the monitor acts as a customer and checks the whole transaction completes (send mail, buy an item, check DB records). Case: Mailping (AT&T/Lucent) — relayed mail off 80+ servers and measured delivery time.
  • Application response time monitoring: measures speed as the end user sees it; historical chart + threshold alert. Extension of end-to-end testing.
  • Scaling:
    • Problem: a polling run not finished before the next starts. Case: WebTV outgrew MRTG → wrote Cricket.
    • Fix: remote probes send summaries to a master station (holds master config). Best method for both types: several data gatherers + one central master.
    • Too many "red" items → need finer priority granularity than green/yellow/red.
    • Dependency chains: one upstream outage floods alerts; system should send one alert naming the root cause.
    • Let an SA "claim" a problem (via ticket system) to avoid uncoordinated fixing.
  • Metamonitoring = monitoring your monitoring system. Simplest form: it monitors itself (e.g., own disk filling). For critical sites, a second system monitors the primary. Should be based on an SLA (e.g., alert if probes haven't run in 10 minutes). Historical metamonitoring predicts when the system can't meet its SLA.

Conclusion: Both types are required at e-commerce sites (customers just go elsewhere). Monitoring is a project that keeps increasing in scope.

Chapter 23

Email Service

Email is a utility service (like dial tone or power) and often the only application the CEO uses. Nearly 45% of business-critical information lives in email. Reliability is the focal point; scalability is a close second. It depends on good namespaces, security architecture, monitoring, and backups.

Foundations: a simple, clear, well-documented architecture, open protocols and standards, and the rule that nothing is a service until it is monitored.

23.1.1 Privacy Policy

  • Every site must have an email privacy policy, communicated to and acknowledged by every employee.
  • It must state when and by whom email may be read; that admins may inadvertently see email while diagnosing; and that email crossing the Internet is not private (don't send unencrypted confidential info).
  • Upper management decides the policy; SAs implement it.

23.1.2 Namespaces

  • The email namespace is the most visible namespace (to customers and employees).
  • Most fundamental rule: use the same email address for internal and external email.
  • The book discourages first.last addresses (e.g., John.Smith) — name collisions force changes that invalidate business cards. Eric Allman (Sendmail creator) opposed full names as addresses since they are not unique.
  • Preferred: unique corporate-wide name tokens (chogan, tal, jsmith) + a directory service for lookup.
  • Tokens: user may choose (default from initials/name), hard to change, and not reused for a couple of months (prevents hijacking a departed employee's mail).

23.1.3 Reliability

  • Failures cause stress, support calls, missed contracts, lost customers.
  • Email is not a place to experiment — deploy only after extensive testing.
  • Case Email Service Beta Test — a company moved 100,000 employees to one server farm; network overloaded, no email for nearly a month; then beta-tested new software on it; rogue servers appeared.
  • Use RAID; ideally hot spares; if not, a rapid restore plan.

23.1.4 Simplicity

Complexity decreases reliability. Limit the number of machines; never involve desktops in mail delivery.

Five main aspects of an email service:

Aspect What it does
Mail transport (MTA) Moves mail server to server
Mail delivery (MDA) Receives and stores mail at the destination server
Access Access protocols (POP3, IMAP4) let the MUA (client) read mail
List processing Delivers one message to a group
Filtering Anti-spam and anti-virus
  • Small site: all functions on one machine (+ maybe an Internet-facing relay). Large site: separate transport, delivery, list processing, ideally all the same type of system.
  • Clients send mail via a relay; disable SMTP on machines not part of the service (use relay-only configs).
  • Case Bad Mail-Delivery Scheme — mail delivered to any host, From: showed the host; replies got "lost." Fix: hostname masquerading (central relay rewrites the sender address to hide the machine name).
  • Avoid gateways (translate between formats) — they add complexity and strip delivery history.
  • Provide a single mechanism for mailing lists.
  • Moving mailboxes between servers: use DNS aliases like username.pobox.example.com so clients don't need reconfiguring.

23.1.5 Spam and Virus Blocking

  • Spam = UCE (unsolicited commercial email); often >50% of incoming Internet mail.
  • Best to block at the server, not the client — easier to upgrade; users disable client protection.
  • No perfect antispam software — it is an arms race. New viruses are nearly impossible to detect.
  • Centralize filtering; outside services can filter via the DNS MX record (privacy concerns).

23.1.6 Generality

  • Use open, universally accepted protocols. SMTP must be supported and should be the only transport protocol; new protocols are likely extensions like ESMTP.
  • Support one or two standard client protocols → almost all mail clients work. Proprietary protocols lock customers in.
  • Case Nonstandard Protocols Are Expensive — acquired start-up had to buy a Windows PC for every UNIX/Mac user to use the parent's proprietary email.

23.1.7 Automation

  • Account creation/deletion should automatically create/remove the mailbox and list memberships.
  • Automate moving accounts between servers (load balancing).
  • Don't auto-forward mail for people who left — use an auto-reply giving the new address and saying they left.
  • Let customers manage their own mailing lists.
  • Periodic checks: accounts vs personnel database; no forwarding outside; disabled accounts off lists; unauthorized members on sensitive lists.

23.1.8 Basic Monitoring

  • Each machine responds to ping and to its TCP ports: SMTP 25, POP3 110, IMAP4 143.
  • Monitor disk space, disk activity, CPU; keep a baseline of normal.
  • Monitor mail to postmaster — receives bounces with full headers and error messages.
  • Monitor logs for message-flow rate.

23.1.9 Redundancy

  • Relay and list-processing hosts: easy — DNS MX records + multiple identically configured servers.
  • Delivery hosts: make internally redundant (RAID) or use shared storage (NAS/SAN) with careful locking.
  • Client access: clients cache DNS, so redundancy must be at the IP level — load balancers (layer 4) or VRRP (Virtual Router Redundancy Protocol).

23.1.10 Scaling

  • Scale in advance of demand. Three variables drive traffic: message size, messages per person, number of people.
  • Message size jumps with new technology (images, documents, audio/video).
  • Mail spool rule of thumb: peak = 2× regular use.
  • Message size limits only as a temporary stopgap (or a really huge limit).
  • Case The Wrong Way to Scale — SA publicly blamed users whose clients auto-checked mail; should have monitored and fixed the root cause.
  • POP3 vs IMAP4:
POP3 IMAP4
Mail location Downloaded to client, server deletes copy Stays on server (all folders)
Server disk Minimal Much greater
Connections Short-lived Long-lived, many simultaneous
Backups On client machines Centrally controlled, predictable
  • List servers must handle unavailable destinations without delaying others.

23.1.11 Security Issues

  • External mail relays are prime targets (exposed to Internet, access to internal network). Design security in from the start.
  • Content scanning for viruses — check against privacy policy; must understand many formats (zip); plan for high disk I/O; scan desktops too.
  • Prevent unauthorized relaying; provide secure remote email access (no plaintext passwords).

23.1.12 Communication

  • Tell customers the policies (privacy, forwarding off-site, backups, filtering, acceptable use) and risks (viruses, chain letters).
  • Brief new hires verbally or with slides; some use web forms to acknowledge policies.
  • Document for SAs: failover/recovery, design diagrams of mail flow, preliminary debugging steps.

23.2 The Icing

  • Encryption: especially for senior managers; must be quick, easy, integrated in the client, and equally easy for recipients to decrypt. Needs key management (key repository, revocation, key recovery). Beware "snake oil" vendors. Sarbanes-Oxley: company must be able to recover the private key.
  • Email Retention Policy: many companies don't back up email or discard backups quickly because in a lawsuit, backups are subpoenaed and searching them is very expensive. A document retention policy gets extended to email (often for SOX compliance). POP3 mail on laptops complicates this.
  • Advanced Monitoring: for revenue-critical email, use end-to-end monitoring that emulates a real customer sending mail.
  • High-Volume List Processing: lists run centrally on servers; can be protected (moderated, members-only) or open; delegate control to list owners; need a list policy (work vs non-work lists). High-volume lists → commercial software or an experienced consultant; build across redundant systems; monitor elapsed time from first to last recipient.
Chapter 24

Print Service

Customers rank printing as one of the most critical services, second only to email. Printing is a utility; it should always work. Many SAs look down on printing, but SA priorities must align with customers' — business still runs on paper (signed contracts, proofreading). The chapter focuses on invariants: getting ink on the page, policies, print server design, and environment. Without policy, design has no direction; without design, printing is unstable.

24.1.1 Level of Centralization

  • Primary requirement: people can print to any printer they have permission to use.
  • Cost recovery: per page, a "tax," or each group funds its own.
  • Typical: one printer per section (hallway, wing, floor); private printers for confidentiality, ego, or business reasons.
  • Trade-off: cost vs convenience. Example: 10 people × $100 desktop printers = $1,000 = a good shared network printer; with 20 people it's better quality at half the price.
  • Case No Standards for Printers — no control on purchases under $600 → dozens of models, many ended up at employees' homes; cost controls fixed it, though employees hated it.

24.1.2 Print Architecture Policy (written policies every site needs)

Policy Key points
General printer architecture How centralized: people per printer, who gets personal printers, how printers are networked
Print spool Spooler = device that receives jobs and holds them until the printer is ready. Also does access control, reroutes around broken printers. One spooler = single point of failure → hot standby/redundant spoolers
Maintenance In-house, time & materials, or service contract; when replacing is cheaper than repairing
Accounting Who pays for printers, repairs, supplies. Simplest: central budget. Per-page billing (universities) discourages waste. Goal: least-objectionable solution
Supplies Who orders/refills. Book recommends against customers changing toner (error-prone, wasteful; operators can shake cartridge for more pages)
Printer equipment standard Part 1 (rarely changes): PostScript vs PCL, duplexing, protocol (LPD, SMB, AppleTalk, USB). Part 2: list of recommended printers/configs, updated regularly. Gives volume discounts, fewer supply types
Printer access Who can print to and cancel jobs on which printers. Book's office stance: everyone can print anywhere and cancel any job (replaces faxing)
Printer naming Name printers geographically (by location/room), not themes like decaf, latte, mocha. Don't name after the group that uses it
  • Case Recommended Configurations Save SA Time — groups bought the right model but forgot the duplexer, cable, network kit.
  • Case An "Open" Cancellation Policy (Bell Labs) — peer pressure prevented abuse; first person to notice a runaway job could cancel it.
  • Location naming pitfall: if the printer moves, everyone must reconfigure → use designated printer areas or floor + north/south names.
  • UNIX two-part names: 2t408-d (duplex) vs 2t408-s (single-sided).

24.1.3 System Design

Peer-to-peer Central funnel
How All hosts spool directly to the printer All hosts send jobs to a central server that distributes them
Pros Simplest to set up (just need printer IP/name); modern printers have reliable built-in spoolers Higher control: format conversion (PostScript ↔ PCL), per-page billing, intelligent printer selection ("any color printer"); drivers updated in one place
Cons Hardest to administer; changes must reach every client; hard to find who sent a rogue job Single point of failure → use savings for two redundant spoolers (manual or automatic failover)
  • Peer-to-peer chaos can be reduced with automated client updates (NIS, cfengine, LPRng).
  • Every added spooler increases admin work. Manual failover is OK if well documented and SAs are trained.

24.1.4 Documentation — three kinds for customers

  1. How-to-print document — menus/commands to connect and print (simplex, duplex, letterhead); rarely changes; in the getting-started manual.
  2. List of printers — catalog of printers, locations, special features; updated whenever printers change; posted near every printer.
  3. Printer label — on every printer showing its name(s); label special trays. Users see this as the most important documentation.

Example: Google's http://print — printers by location with floorplan maps, setup instructions, queue view, ticket link.

24.1.5 Monitoring

Nothing deserves the name service until it is monitored. Two aspects:

  1. The spooling/print service — queue not stalled, spool disk not full or dead, logs recycled, CPU not overloaded.
  2. Each printer's status — most speak SNMP to report jams, low toner, no paper. No automated way to know whether spare paper is near the printer → visit or deputize customers.

Case: Print Tests — weekly Monday test page on every printer; an operator checked toner and restocked paper.

24.1.6 Environmental Issues

  • Toner is toxic; printing kills trees and fills landfills. A page not printed has lower impact than one printed and recycled.
  • Recycle toner cartridges; put recycling bins next to each trash can and printer (people recycle when it's easy). Coordinate with facilities.
  • Give tools: preview tools, default to duplex, no burst (cover) pages, web-based paperless forms.
  • Case Creating a Recycling Program — company ignored vendor's cartridge-return incentive because nobody created a procedure. Lesson: "These things don't create themselves. Take the initiative."

24.2 The Icing ("Rolls-Royce" service)

  • Automatic Failover and Load Balancing: two spoolers each handle half the printers and substitute for each other. Failover = detection + cutover. Detection is hard (a spooler may answer pings yet not print); avoid false positives; tell server down vs printer down apart. Most problems are on the PC side (drivers, apps).
  • Outsourced printing: for rare needs (binding, wide formats) send PDFs to a print shop — cheaper than a rarely used printer.
  • Dedicated Clerical Support: a clerk/operator visits printers, refills, arranges repairs — cheaper than an SA; give them exact toner order codes.
  • Shredding: shred anything you wouldn't want on the front page of the New York Times. On-site shredding (truck at your site) is preferred, with someone watching; spot-check off-site services. Not printing is more secure than shredding.
  • Dealing with Printer Abuse: "You can't solve social problems using technology." Define abuse in the acceptable-use policy; per-page billing or free allotments; "top-ten printers" shaming backfired (people competed). Best: balanced, nontechnical approach — put printers in very visible public places; address real waste personally, don't be petty.
  • Case The 500-Page Printout (game cheats) — director emailed everyone asking the owner to pick it up from his office; never claimed; nothing more needed to be said.
Chapter 27

Remote Access Service

A remote access service gives authorized individuals access to the company network from home, customer sites, or anywhere. It went from a niche for technical people to a core service everyone uses (telecommuters, road warriors).

Two main categories: (1) connect directly into the network (dial-up modems, ISDN); (2) connect to the Internet (WiFi, cable, DSL) then tunnel/VPN into the network. The chapter teaches how to evaluate, not which technology to pick (tech changes too fast).

Basics in one line: understand requirements → agree on service levels → outsource as much as possible, but keep authentication, authorization, and perimeter security in-house.

27.1.1 Requirements for Remote Access

Unstated first requirement: everyone must have a low-cost, convenient remote access solution. Otherwise customers build their own (less secure) and SAs end up supporting it anyway.

User group Needs
Travelers checking email Short connections from anywhere → technology must be ubiquitous; know the coverage area and cost model
Evening/weekend catch-up from home Short, reliable, economical connections
Regular work-from-home (1) Reliable (outages hurt daily work), (2) high-speed, (3) different economics — 8–10 hrs/day, so always-on is cheaper than per-minute
High-bandwidth Internet (home/conference) Encryption required — unencrypted data on the Internet may count as publication (lose IP rights); never send plaintext passwords. Server side usually at the corporate firewall
At another company's site (consultants, support engineers) Most difficult. Must obey the customer's rules; no always-on line (could bridge the two networks). Use an encrypted channel through the customer's firewall to the company firewall

Because needs differ, a remote access service is usually several components.

27.1.2 Policy for Remote Access

Define before offering the service: acceptable use, security policies, responsibilities of users, who gets what access, and who pays.

27.1.3 Definition of Service Levels

  • Define service levels with customers; failures are highly visible because people can't work. Clear levels set expectations for ETR (estimated time to repair).
  • Lone SA at a small site: negotiate levels that let her sleep. Large org with 24/7 helpdesk: more customer-oriented levels; train helpdesk on when to escalate.
  • Trial services and services being phased out get lower service levels; trial users need a backup access method.
  • Case Choose Customers for Trial Services Carefully — an ISDN trial was forced open to demanding engineers; one escalated an outage to top priority. Lesson: "Major bandwidth increases are addictive" — finish prototyping before early adopters get access; budget projects before they become political disasters.

27.1.4 Centralization

Remote access benefits from centralization: authentication must be centralized (auditing, access control); research costs on new tech shouldn't be duplicated; economies of scale. The ultimate form of centralization is outsourcing.

27.1.5 Outsourcing

Best way to handle constantly changing technology: get someone else (usually an ISP) to do it.

  • Benefits: much less support time; cost becomes visible and predictable (good for budgeting); fewer after-hours calls; no modem pool to support; vendor's evaluations are funded since it's their core business.
  • Never outsource the authentication database — keep it in-house for the exit process, auditing, and discreet sensitive terminations.

Vendor evaluation criteria (memorize):

  1. Coverage area — homes + travel areas; prefer global with option for smaller areas. "Fully covered" = local rates or free to call.
  2. Supported technologies — and their rate of adoption (or customers build their own again).
  3. First-level support — does the vendor provide it, or only via escalation from SAs?
  4. Service-level agreement — get a written SLA: response times, nonperformance clauses, tracking, speed/latency.
  5. Billing structure — ideally all costs in the vendor's charges (no expense reports); bill per department.
  6. Authentication interface — standard protocols; company manages the auth database.
  7. Security — company's security team is still responsible; check vendor's security architecture.
  8. End-to-end bandwidth — estimate peak users × bandwidth each; VPN terminators need enough CPU (encryption is CPU-intensive).

Choose carefully: changing remote access vendors is difficult (reconfigure every machine, new lines).

27.1.6 Authentication

  • Always built and maintained in-house, even if everything else is outsourced.
  • All remote access methods use the same authentication database (less overhead; none overlooked when disabling departed employees).
  • Use one-time password / token-based systems, not reusable passwords. Small companies may start with passwords but should budget to switch.

27.1.7 Perimeter Security

  • Remote access is part of the company's perimeter, even if outsourced. Misconfigured dynamic routing can create a back door.
  • Case Restrict Traffic and Routing Across Remote Access Links — a married couple working for competitors; both companies' home links routed through their home network, so Company B's mail for Company A crossed the couple's house. Fix: restrict routes and limit traffic to the one authorized host.

27.2 The Icing

  • Home Office: remote access is only part of teleworking. Decide who provides and supports home equipment (home visits are expensive → specify in SLA, recover costs from the department). Home workers need phone, fax, print, conference calls — consider company-billed lines (expense reports are a hidden cost). Combat feeling out of touch with video conferencing and broadcasting meetings.
  • Cost Analysis and Reduction: costs are hidden from those who incur them. Move people from toll-free to local numbers; give heavy fixed-location users always-on links; automate notifications. Case: Lucent billing analysis — 1-800 number routed to nearest modem pool; a dynamic sign-on banner told callers the cheaper number; monthly emails showed money they could have saved; top 10% savers moved to VPN first.
  • New Technologies: supporting old + new tech raises costs; modems are backward-compatible, others aren't. Rule: support at most two technologies plus traditional modem dial-in. When adding a new one, aggressively phase out the older with a hard cutover date.

Conclusion: remote access is time-consuming and thankless; define policy, agree on service levels, outsource, keep authentication in-house, and limit the number of technologies.

Chapter 28

Software Depot Service

A software depot is a way of making a large number of software packages available to many hosts. Without one, nontechnical users lack tools and technical users install their own, duplicating effort. A good depot makes the most anemic OS rich and useful — so much that users forget it isn't part of the vendor's OS (Case: We Forget How Bare Vendor Installations Can Be — wget on Solaris).

UNIX depot Windows depot
Nature Software used in real time from the depot Software to be installed
Typical form Shared file server mounted at e.g. /sw or /opt/net, add to $PATH; export read-only (1) network disk with software that runs from it; (2) software push system (e.g., MS-SMS); (3) distribution server of .ZIP/.EXE packages for manual install
Why Installs are complex, need root; one team builds for all hosts Registry settings must be set by an installer

28.1.1 Understand the Justification

  • A depot is a customer service that finds, installs, and maintains a library of software; depot maintainers are librarians.
  • Benefits: reduces duplication of effort, saves searching, acts as a bandwidth cache, consolidates purchases, leverages installation expertise, ensures security/bug-fix updates reach everyone.
  • Consistency → machines become interchangeable (choose hosts by hardware, not by installed software).
  • Even small sites need a consistent software layout (otherwise a "rat's nest"). Warning signs you need a real depot: micromanaging the software library, duplicating builds, inconsistent packages.

28.1.2 Understand the Technical Expectations

Base the depot on users' requirements (may need multiple versions of a tool at once). Consider reliability: local files vs remote server; use replication or RAID for higher availability.

28.1.3 Set the Policy

Letting anyone contribute is dangerous (Trojan horse = malicious program with the same name as another program; accidental incompatible upgrades). The policy answers:

  • Who may build and install packages?
  • What if the maintainer leaves?
  • Which OSs are supported? (One depot for all with wrapper scripts — a script that detects the platform and calls the right binary — or one per OS?)
  • Is there a standard wrapper template?
  • How are upgrades, bugs, and deletions handled?
  • Scope of distribution — cluster, department, enterprise?
  • How do customers request packages? (depot committee?)

Publish the policy where customers will see it.

28.1.4 Select Depot Software

  • UNIX: select an existing depot system; don't write one from scratch (customizing is easier). Examples: Depot (seminal), LUDE, Modules, SEPP, GNU Stow (manages symbolic links).
  • Windows: a network share, FTP server, or website.
  • If a legacy repository exists, decide whether to augment or replace it.

28.1.5 Create the Process Manual

Document the local procedure for adding packages (with local hostnames etc.). Later write a short quick guide with cut-and-paste commands so experienced SAs don't skip steps.

28.1.6 Examples

UNIX example (/sw depot):

  • Build on a documented build machine per OS; source in /home/src/\; a SOURCEME script sets up the build environment (promotes institutional learning).
  • Install in versioned directories: /sw/perl-6.0, /sw/perl-6.1 → supports multiple versions.
  • /sw/perl = symbolic link to the latest supported release. Test a new version by not updating the link until it's certified; running processes keep using the old version.
  • /sw/default/bin = links to popular programs → users add one directory to PATH. GNU Stow creates minimal links.
  • Networked via NFS + automounter maps per OS (e.g., Solaris 8 on bester, Solaris 9 on lyta); obsolete packages are not carried forward to a new OS unless requested. Keep current + one previous OS release actively maintained.
  • Automate with a master file + make that generates automounter maps and Stow commands (humans are bad at keeping things in sync).
  • Delete conservatively: rename first, delete after about a week, announce it. Disk use is self-limiting as old OSs retire.
  • Packages read-only to non-privileged users. Customers hand software to an SA (archives source centrally).
  • /sw/contrib = area where customers install and share their own software; owner is responsible; don't put /sw/contrib in PATH — link from $HOME/bin; rules in /sw/contrib/POLICY.
  • The book prefers no wrappers — they're a slippery slope.
  • System is self-documenting (master file + layout).

Windows example (\\server1\software share) with a POLICY document and directories:

Directory Contents
Standard Approved for all machines but not installed by default; first place to look
Preinstalled Already on new PCs / pushed by MS-SMS; kept for reinstalls
Disk images CD/DVD images licensed or freely distributable (e.g., Linux ISOs); saves Internet bandwidth
Experimental Not yet approved, under evaluation; ACL/password restricted
Admin SA-only tools and specially licensed software; SA installs to enforce licenses
  • Also an Obsolete directory; one directory per package with clear names; a .txt notes file per directory (license, install tips).
  • Windows depots are light on the network (install once), so often not replicated — just put on a well-connected server. Slow NFS to a UNIX depot destroys productivity.

28.2 The Icing

  • Different Configurations for Different Hosts: config file in depot is a symlink to a local file (e.g., /etc/megasoft.conf) which may link back to standard configs in the depot → bounce links.
  • Local Replication: cache popular packages on local disk (less network, faster) — hard to track manually. "Better the right thing happen slowly than the wrong thing with excellent performance." Affects backup policy. Best general solution: an NFS cache (e.g., Solaris cachefs) — adaptive, set-it-and-forget-it.
  • Commercial Software in the Depot: site license → include normally; software that checks a license server → make available to all. Otherwise SA must enforce the license (SA installs, install on licensed host only, or a UNIX group with execute permission). Different license servers per group → bounce links.
  • Second-Class Citizens: off-beat OSs with minimal support (maybe just an IP address). Give them a minimal depot — tools for the machine's purpose (e.g., compiler tool chain), SA process tools (inventory, log rotation, backups, debugging), and a few convenience tools. Don't provide every package. Have a written policy on support level. Tool chain = software needed to build software (make, compilers, assemblers, linkers, debuggers, source control) — one tool leads to the next.
Chapter 29

Web Services

A web site presents information and applications using a client/server model; the client is a browser (any program speaking HTTP). The server delivers pages in HTML plus embedded content; some content (e.g., JavaScript) runs in the browser.

  • The web is built on open standards — W3C defines web standards, IETF defines Internet protocols; no royalties.
  • The web browser is a universal client — new services need no new client software.
  • Case The Power of Open Standards — Network Notes (AT&T, Lotus, Novell) failed: proprietary software, no sites, no users. Lock-in locks potential customers out.

29.1.1 Web Service Building Blocks

  • URL (uniform resource locator) = address of information on the web (hostname + path).
  • Web server = receives HTTP requests and replies with data (Apache, AOLServer, Microsoft IIS).
  • CGI (Common Gateway Interface) = generates dynamic content on demand (often from a database).
  • GET = read-only (inputs in the URL). POST = update/mutation (inputs inside the request; can be longer). Search engines don't follow POSTs — famous bug: a DELETE button made as a GET → web crawler deleted all data.
  • In-line interpreted code: PHP, Microsoft ASP.
  • AJAX = Asynchronous JavaScript and XML — a technique (not a protocol) for interactive pages; contacts the server only when needed.
  • Formats: HTML/XHTML/XML; streaming for real-time audio/video; RSS feed = an XML microformat listing a site's table of contents.

HTTP status codes (1xx info, 2xx success, 3xx redirect, 4xx error):

Code Meaning
200 OK — request completed
301 Moved permanently (remember the new URL)
302 Redirect to specified URL
307 Redirect temporarily
401 Try again with authentication (browser first requests without auth, gets 401, then resends with credentials)
403 Unauthorized / wrong password / forbidden
404 No such page found (returns an error page, can be customized)

29.1.2 The Webmaster Role

  • Webmaster = manages the content of a web site (like a newspaper editor) and sets web site policy.
  • Web system administrator = maintains the technical machinery (server, software).
  • Often confused (same person at small sites). Reinforce the difference: different departments or budgets.
  • If forced into webmaster duty: enable self-service updates; get an SLA for update requests (e.g., major updates Monday, minor within 8 hours, emergency process).

29.1.3 Service-Level Agreements

  • Web services need an SLA + monitoring. Most internal web services share the SLA of other office services (printing, storage). Set the SLA with customers.
  • Resist SLAs with no room for maintenance unless redundant.
  • Key metric: latency at a given QPS (queries per second) — measured from first byte of the request to last byte of the answer.

29.1.4 Web Service Architectures

Type Description Key resource at high QPS
Static web server Serves documents that don't change, read straight from disk, out of the document root RAM (to cache common files)
CGI server Generates pages dynamically; serves fewer pages/sec CPU
Database-driven site Uses templates filled from a database (e.g., bookstore catalog); global change = edit the template. Encourage sites to move to this model early Scale like any database
Multimedia server Large media files, streaming (non-HTTP protocol), DRM, rate control, buffering Storage, network bandwidth, memory, CPU (real-time re-encoding → special hardware cards)
  • LAMP = Linux, Apache, MySQL, Perl/PHP/Python — naming platforms reduces confusion.
  • Multiple servers on one host: (1) different port (e.g., :8001) — users forget the port; (2) multiple IP addresses / virtual interfaces (VIFs) per group — more work up front but scales nicely (move a group's IP to another host).

29.1.5 Monitoring

Use Chapter 22 methods plus web-specific items: web server errors (content/script problems or intrusion attempts), browser client type, referring URL, active threads/memory.

29.1.6 Scaling for Web Services

"Scaling is the only problem on the Internet. Everything else is a sub-problem." — Mike O'Dell (UUNET). "Slashdot effect" = a site mentioned on a popular site gets overloaded.

Horizontal scaling Vertical scaling
Meaning Replicate a resource and divide the load (e.g., 2 identical web servers) Separate subservices onto different machines; more resources at the busiest stages
How Round-robin DNS (drawbacks: clients cache IPs, keeps giving out a dead server) or better a hardware load balancer (tracks down servers, routes to least busy) e.g., move video clips to their own server so the survey database isn't competing
  • Strata's case: load balancer timed out idle LDAP connections without a TCP FIN → mysterious failures.
  • Choosing a method: classify components by the resources they use most; fix the most overloaded component first, one at a time (easier testing and budgeting).
  • Scaling challenges: state (keep in browser or shared; modern load balancers track sessions; NAT complicates it); replace CGI lock files with a database; split databases into read-only (replicated) and read-write views.
  • Case The Importance of Scaling — Florida Election Board site overloaded in 2000 election; whole department lost Internet.
  • Typical progression: static + dynamic + database on one machine → each on its own machine → scale each individually (static on NFS/SAN; dedicated dynamic servers; separate databases).

29.1.7 Web Service Security

Intruders often don't deface — they use your resources: warez, spam, attacking others, bot farms. Even internal web services must be secured (viruses, HR/finance data, visitors, compromised networks, accidental DoS). Most important: stay up to date on new threats.

Secure connections and certificates

  • HTTPS = HTTP over SSL; prevents eavesdropping (e.g., coffee-shop WiFi).
  • Certificate has a public half (given to anyone) and private half (must stay secret — leaked = others can impersonate your site). Keep a key escrow (e.g., USB key in a safe).
  • Storing the private key on the web server is risky → store it encrypted, requiring a password at every restart (or an obfuscated password + protected helper program).
  • Self-signed certificate (e.g., made with OpenSSL) encrypts but can't prove identity → browser warning. CA (certification authority)-signed certificate proves the site is genuine; hierarchy of trust CA → your cert → browser.
  • Crypto is CPU-intensive (500 plain QPS vs ~100 SSL QPS) → SSL accelerators; fine as long as the server is network-bound before CPU-bound.

Protecting the content — common attacks

  • Directory traversal — requesting files outside the document root (e.g., ../../../some-file).
  • Form-field corruption — altering a copied web form (e.g., changing a hidden price field, even to negative). Store a product ID and look up the price server-side.
  • SQL injection — a variant of form-field corruption: input that makes SQL always true or runs arbitrary queries/commands.

Application security disciplines

  1. Limit the potential damage — golden master copy internally; isolate the web server; connections initiated from inside only.
  2. Validate input — check length (buffer overflows); validate by inclusion, not exclusion (allow only a set of characters — don't block O'Brien's apostrophe).
  3. Automate data access — read-only DB access when possible; stored procedures; prepared statements.
  4. Use permissions and privileges — least privilege; XSRF is an example of misused permissions.
  5. Use logging — protection of last resort; store logs off-machine or in nonstandard places (network logging to a central log host).

29.1.8 Content Management

  • SAs should not do content updates — it creates a bottleneck and the problem grows (no incentive to hire a webmaster).
  • Web team: content management belongs to the people who create content (sales, marketing, PR). Best: a web team serving internal + external sites. Smaller orgs: a web council = webmaster + representative from each stakeholder group (incl. systems staff).
  • Case Will They Really Read It This Weekend? — Friday "urgent" marketing updates. Fix: start a web council → visibility → support for hiring a webmaster.
  • Change control (owned by the web council) distinguishes three alterations:
    • Update = add new material or replace with a newer version
    • Change = alter site structure (new directory, redirect links)
    • Fix = correct content or behavior that doesn't meet standards (needs a ticket + QA)
  • Case Policy + Automation = Less Politics — Tom set up www-draft → www-qa → www (live), then self-service push scripts with sudo to take SAs out of the updates and the politics.

29.1.9 Building the Manageable Generic Web Server

Questions to ask the requester: internal or Internet? for a specific application? who uses it? uptime requirements (maintenance window)? accounts/groups? storage? expected traffic and growth?

  • Any site: plan the URL namespace (e.g., /cgi-bin, /images, /user/$USER); avoid ID numbers in URLs (confidential). A shared URL is expected to last forever → use redirects. Separate config includes per section so web teams can't break sitewide settings. Don't let people run their own CGIs by default (set resource limits if you must). Let requesters update their own content; ideally database-driven.
  • Internal (intranet) site: shared document root, each group gets read/write on its own subdirectory; groups that modify the server itself get a separate (virtual) server.
  • External site: good security (block unused ports, firewall). Four independent pieces: domain registration, Internet DNS hosting, web hosting, web content.
  • Web production process: three identical servers — draft → QA → live (production). Optional golden master server: identical to production but hidden/internal, for auditing and integration testing.

29.2 The Icing

  • Third-Party Web Hosting: a company provides web servers; managed hosting adds services like monitoring.
    • Advantages: "Integration is more powerful than invention"; no local install; dashboard; often better reliability and security than you can provide (economies of scale); helps disaster recovery and scaling; best for small sites ($5/month and up).
    • Disadvantages: worrying about the data (mitigated by exports/hosted backups), difficulty letting go, fear that outsourcing hosting leads to outsourcing the SA.
  • Unified Login / Managing Profiles: one password system for all apps (too many passwords → sticky notes on monitors). Profile = login/password + access/customization info. Methods: .htaccess/.htpasswd, LDAP/Active Directory, PAM, SQL lookups. Choose products that integrate with your authentication system; ideally the app uses the pre-authenticated username from the web server.
  • Mashup Applications: a web site that uses data and APIs from other sites to make a new application (e.g., HousingMaps = Google Maps + Craigslist). Two parts to scale: the glue and the services used. Good APIs use registration keys with rate limits (QPS and daily max) stated in an SLA. Exceeding limits = abuse or unexpected success — smart companies don't assume abuse. A mashup shows you have valuable data; consult legal and marketing before blocking.
Chapter 31

Perception and Visibility

Good system administration is like good theater: the audience sees the show, not the backstage work, but values it more when it understands what went into it.

Perception Visibility
How people see you How much people see of you
A measure of quality A measure of quantity
Covered in The Basics Covered in The Icing
  • Customers' perceptions of you are their reality of you. If they don't know you exist, you don't exist; with a vacuum of information, they assume the worst.
  • Doing the technical work well is not enough to be perceived well.
  • You are responsible for how you are perceived — nobody else will do it for you.

31.1.1 A Good First Impression

  • The first impression dominates all future interactions. Bad start = "the bad kid" all year; good start = occasional mistakes seen as a fluke.
  • Goodwill bank account: every good deed is a deposit; you can survive about one bad thing for every five good things.
  • At appointments: be on time, polite, friendly, willing to listen; smile. Dress appropriately for your company's culture (Case: Chromatically Different Hair — pink hair made customers assume she was technically excellent). Case: The First Time I Met You — "First impressions are lasting impressions."
  • Unruffled exterior is the SA's greatest asset. Don't yell — if about to, excuse yourself to the restroom.
  • New hires will eventually be most of your customers: have their computer, accounts, and everything working on day one; a productive first day keeps motivation high.
  • Case S-l-o-w PC Delivery — a programmer had no computer for a month and coworkers thought it normal.
  • Case PC Delivery at NYIT — new PCs arrive on a cart with jingle bells; crowd gathers, applause → positive visibility.
  • Get computing needs on the secretaries' new-hire checklist (computer, network jack, login name, mailing lists, software, accounts).
  • On day one, the friendliest SA gives an in-person orientation and a printed "welcome to our network" guide — puts a face on the SA team (customers don't like faceless organizations).
  • Case Orientation Sessions — if SAs don't orient new hires, someone else will (and may bad-mouth the SAs).

31.1.2 Attitude, Perception, and Customers

  • People perceive you by the attitude you project.
  • Number-one SA attitude problem: blatant disrespect for the people they serve ("lusers," "pests with requests"). They are the reason SAs have jobs; SAs and users are on the same team.
  • Call them "customers," not "users" (Smallwood 1992) — reminds SAs they are in a service industry. Working as a consultant teaches this.
  • But "the customer is always right" goes too far — SAs must politely say no when appropriate and reject requests against policy. "Just because I can do it doesn't mean I should." Help customers help themselves.
  • Higher roles (architect) → a "business partners" relationship with a scope of work.
  • Treat every problem as a fun puzzle, not a burden. "Users are stupid"? They know more about their own field than you.
  • Case Venting About Customers — SA called customers "idiots" in the cafeteria with their director at the next table; he was removed. Vent privately to your manager; email can be forwarded.
  • End ticket replies with sincere thanks for reporting the problem.

31.1.3 Priorities Aligned with Customer Expectations

  • Customers judge efficiency not by how hard you work but by how soon their requests are completed.
  • Customers expect small things done quickly and big things in reasonable time — by their definition of big/small.
  • Priority order: (1) emergencies, (2) quick requests, (3) longer requests. Same work, different order → delighted customers. Don't let big jobs be postponed endlessly.
  • Password resets are urgent due to the domino effect (can't log in = can't work).
  • Make quick requests self-serve (web password reset, DHCP, a tear-off pad of IP addresses, toner kept near the printer).
  • Organization-wide: front-line handles quick requests, second tier longer ones, senior SAs projects.
  • Two-SA teams: mutual-interruption shield — one SA shields the other in the morning, and vice versa in the afternoon.

31.1.4 The System Advocate

System clerk System advocate
Reactive Proactive
Told what to do on a schedule; not involved in planning Advocates customers' needs to management; involved in planning
Does clerical tasks manually (backups, accounts) Automates clerical tasks
  • Becoming an advocate is a slow evolution that starts with one proactive project (time now, savings later).
  • Benefits: aligns with customers; builds a can-do reputation → promotions and raises.
  • Clerks are where SAs start and learn; better to be managed by SAs than by customers.

Examples

  • Installing software: clerk gets the media late, hits license/disk problems, takes weeks. Advocate is involved from the start, does capacity planning, agrees a timetable. "People value what they understand and devalue what they don't."
  • Solving a performance problem: clerk just installs a faster network card (can make it worse — floods the server). Advocate diagnoses, explains options, and lets the customer present the proposal to management.
  • Simple automation: clerk batches account creation weekly; advocate automates it. Automate the 80% and document special cases. A script can output the commands for review. Automation also prevents errors.
  • Automating anything: first do the entire process manually, record the steps, then automate those exact steps; test each step separately as you go.

31.2 The Icing — Increasing Visibility

SA visibility paradox: SAs are noticed only when something breaks. 100% uptime makes management think SAs aren't needed. Don't keep systems unstable — show your value subtly. Nobody will promote you except yourself. Visibility only works if you already do good work — no point advertising a bad product.

  • System Status Web Page: current status, time/date stamped, how to report problems, monitoring links, planned outages, recent changes. Make it the default browser page with useful content (weather, news). Shows you care and reduces redundant calls ("Server sinclair is down; we're working on it"). Must be updated consistently. Low-tech alternative: a whiteboard at the machine room (+ webcam).
  • Management Meetings: 30 minutes every 2 weeks with each customer group's manager — shows projects, lets them prioritize, and educates them on invisible infrastructure work.
  • Physical Visibility: hidden office = inaccessible; fully visible = every break looks like slacking. Customer-facing SAs in visible spots; back-office architects less visible. Cases: Office Location (Bell Labs) and Desk Location (Cibernet London — customers reached the senior SA first; Tom rearranged seating).
  • Town Hall Meetings: yearly "state of the network"; different area heads present; plan and script it. User group meetings (monthly/quarterly) = half entertainment, half feedback. Sample agenda: Welcome (2 min) → Introductions (5) → Feedback (20) → Review (10) → Show and tell (30) → Meeting review (5) → Closing (2). During feedback: ask open-ended questions, write key phrases on an easel pad, don't reject or explain away requests (people must feel safe), don't let one person dominate. Close by thanking people.
  • Newsletters: mostly ignored or seen as PR over problem-solving; if you must, keep it simple and useful (FAQ, "ask the SAs"). If you hired a full-time person for it, it's not simple.
  • Mail to All Customers: before major changes; brief; most important info in the first sentence (most read only the subject + first line). Put "ACTION REQUIRED" in the subject when needed. Include two different contact methods (e.g., email + phone). Use only occasionally. Google: start by saying who doesn't need to read it. Les Lloyd used his email signature to promote services.
  • Lunch: eat with different customers regularly. Case: Free Lunch — Tommy Reingold takes the creator of every 10,000th ticket to lunch.

Conclusion: Don't leave perception and visibility to fate — unmanaged, they will be a disaster.

Chapter 32

Being Happy

A happy SA handles stress and an endless workload, looks forward to work, has good relationships with customers, coworkers, and managers, feels in control, and has a good social/family life. Happy SAs share habits: good personal skills, communication skills, self-psychology, and managing their managers. These are learnable skills; rule of thumb: a habit is learned by doing it for a month.

Basics = being organized and communicating well.

32.1.1 Follow-Through

  • Follow-through = completing what you committed to do. Nothing frustrates customers more than dropped requests.
  • Keep one organizer (paper, PDA, electronic) for to-dos and calendar; combine work and social calendars (separate ones get out of sync). Memory is imperfect (Einstein didn't memorize his own phone number).
  • Trouble-ticket systems ensure promises aren't forgotten.
  • Case Dynamic To-Do Lists — Tom keeps 365 lists a year: copies unfinished items forward, tags must-do-today items each morning, renegotiates deadlines, and gets closure every day.
  • Any system is better than no system.

32.1.2 Time Management

Time management = using time wisely. Work smarter, not harder — customers see results, not effort.

  • Why it's hard: SA work is interrupt-driven (like a bus driver stopping for every passenger). Break the cycle: split the day (projects in the morning, requests in the afternoon), trade interrupt duty with a coworker, or come in early.
  • Deflecting interruptions: write the request on your to-do list; if you can't, say so and ask for an email or ticket (even suggest the wording). Don't sound rude.
  • Goal setting: stop "mopping the floor instead of fixing the leak." List goals, prioritize, drop low-priority ones, plan small steps. Review accomplishments on the first day of each month; review status weekly.
  • Daily planning: start each day by reviewing and prioritizing your to-do list (a 5-minute investment). Case: Putting It All Together — Tom checks alerts and urgent mail only, plans the day in 1-hour blocks, then spends the first (uninterrupted) hour on his top-priority item. Find your biological peak hour too.
  • Handling paper once: touch each piece of paper (or email) only once — throw away, act, respond, or file. File as little as possible; when in doubt, throw it out. Use an email archive folder and filters like procmail ("If you aren't using procmail, you're working too hard").
  • Staying focused: clean desk, clean inbox, clean screen (use virtual screens); read email in set blocks.
  • Daily tasks: do must-do-today items first. Case: Schedule Daily Tasks Efficiently — tape changes moved to right after lunch. Never schedule daily tasks at the end of the day.
  • Precompiling decisions: make a decision once (like a compiler optimizer) and use a mantra: It's always a good time to save your work · Always make backups · Record requests · Change tapes M-W-F · Take the PDA · Sooner is better than later.
  • Finding free time: use "light" times of year; eliminate rather than automate; stop reading Usenet; leave your two busiest mailing lists monthly; mail filters; come in an hour early; avoid rush hour; take a time-management class; learn perl and make; meet your chief customer to drop items; hire an assistant (e.g., student) for tactical tasks.
  • Human time wasters (3 steps): (1) coach them tactfully, (2) work with their management, (3) last resort: manage their time — become their highest-priority interrupt (stand in their office).
  • Slow bureaucrats: (1) befriend them — be the pleasant one; (2) give them hundreds of requests at once so it escalates to a manager who can change the process.
  • Training: time-management classes are a small investment with a big payoff.

32.1.3 Communication Skills

All problems can be viewed as communication problems. You don't have to like someone to work with them — just communicate.

Problem type Skill to use
My problems I statements (be heard)
Your problems Active listening (hear correctly)
Our problems All skills — agree on problem + action plan
Other people's problems Discipline: mind your own business
  • I statements: form "I feel [emotion] when you [action]." Express the soft emotion (sadness, fear), not the hard emotion (anger) — anger makes people defensive. Gives a fixable problem statement.
  • Active listening — three tools:
    • Mirroring: repeat back a shorter, accurate version before replying (like checking a packet checksum). Start with "I hear you saying that..." Case: Don't Trust Your Ears — CEO's "smaller team" misunderstanding. Case: "Say Again?" — standard phrase for "repeat that."
    • Summary statements: pause to list the points made so far; group issues to reveal underlying causes; vital at the end of meetings (email it as a record).
    • Reflection: for angry/upset people — name the emotion ("Wow! You are really upset about this!"). Deal with emotions before the technical problem.

32.1.4 Professional Development

  • Training to maintain and improve skills + joining professional organizations. Never turn down the opportunity to learn. Read the trade journal of your customers' industry.
  • One-day seminars = tactical (specific skill). Week-long conferences = strategic (big picture, networking, community).
  • Recommended: USENIX and LOPSA; LISA (Large Installation System Administration) conference.

32.1.5 Staying Technical

Being offered management is a compliment — consider it, but management means giving up technical duties. To stay technical, agree with your boss on the line between "technical lead" (architecting, deploying) and "manager" (budgets, salary/performance reviews, HR). Beware slowly sliding into management.

32.2.1 Learn to Negotiate

Negotiation = the art of getting what you want.

  • Work toward win-win. Win-lose is second best; always avoid lose-lose and lose-win.
  • Recognize you're negotiating — ask "Is that negotiable?" Brief your team on what not to leak (Case: Prepare the Team — start-up leaked its funding deadline). Know the power dynamic — it can shift (Case: Power Can Shift — vendor's year-end quota).
  • Plan: must-haves vs sacrifices, secrets, script the meeting, be specific. Cases: Know Who a Vendor's Competition Is (Sun mug for HP reps); Do Your Homework (stack of mishandled tickets). Hardball tactics only when you'll never negotiate with that person again. Case: Use a Variety of Techniques — one-tactic manager became isolated.
  • Meeting format: define terms → agree on common ground → tackle the hard parts; table slow items.
  • Tips: ask for what you honestly want (don't negotiate against yourself); after an offer, close your mouth ("the next person to talk is the loser"); don't reveal your strategy (ask an agent "Who pays your commission?"); always refuse a first offer (risky; works once).
  • Silence is a key tool; once you reach agreement, stop talking. Case: Be Careful What You Say ("Would you like it to be more?"). Book: The Haggler's Handbook.

32.2.2 Love Your Job

  • Enjoying what you do: if you won the lottery, what would you do? Do that as a career (Office Space). Case: Following Our Own Advice — Christine → Formula 1, Tom → political campaign.
  • Being motivated: money motivates only short term; helping people and compliments are habit-forming motivators. The critical inner voice can poison compliments; shame (bottled-up problems) hurts productivity. Accept compliments — just say "Thank you!" Case: An Unsafe Workplace Is an Unproductive Workplace — a manager ignored a hostile remark and lost a good SA.
  • Happiness: (cognitive theory) depends on how you react to events, not the events. Recognize the critical inner voice and consider the source. Use the EAP (employee assistance program).
  • Good boss/bad boss: your manager affects how much you love your job more than the work itself; most people leave because of their bosses.
  • Accepting criticism: thank the critic; separate constructive vs nonconstructive criticism. Don't "should" yourself — replace "I should have" with "next time, I will."
  • Support structure: people to go to for office-politics, technical, and life advice.
  • Ask for help: it's not a weakness; solve problems while small. Case: Should Have Asked for Help — speaker with transparencies rented a laptop and retyped his talk instead of asking coordinators.
  • Balance work and personal life: avoid burnout; respect family ("Nobody's last words were 'I wish I had spent more time at the office'"), listen to your body, take your vacations — they reveal whether your job is documented and covered.
  • Awards wall: post thank-you notes and awards where you see them daily; email version = a "feathers" folder.

32.2.3 Managing Your Manager

  • Your job is to make your boss a success (your tasks are a fraction of her goals). A successful boss gets promoted and takes you along; managers invest in star performers. Case: Raise-Time Humor.
  • Managing = steering the boat others row. Case: Pleasing the Visionary — frame basic upgrades as steps toward the dean's futuristic goals.
  • Make your needs known (managers can't read minds) but don't pester. Share your career goal once or twice a year. Case: Steer Promotions to You — Tom asked for every promotion.
  • When overloaded: don't complain — bring your to-do list with time estimates and ask your boss to prioritize.
  • Upward delegation: don't delegate busywork upward; delegate things needing the boss's authority that make whole classes of requests go away (e.g., a policy that only supported servers are backed up). Case: Use Your Boss's Power When Needed — non-work PC requests (MIDI cards, game OS). Watch quantity and timing; ask right after receiving a compliment.

Conclusion: happiness and success are interrelated. Finding free time = eliminating time wasters, not managing them better. You deserve to be happy at work.
